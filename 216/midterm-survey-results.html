Summary Statistics for Multiple Choice Questions

The views expressed on Collaboration are fascinating as they sharply contrast past opinions. Fall 2025 were not allowed to use AI nor collaborate with students on Projects. I polled students as they exited CSMC216 about introducing Free Collaboration with AI and other students in future semesters.

  • 46% of Fall 2025 students indicated they would still do projects without relying on AI. Only 19% of Fall 2026 students indicated they didn't use AI on Project 1.
  • 59% of Fall 2025 students indicated they would collaborate with their peers on projects if allowed to do so. Only 29% of Fall 2026 students indicated that they collaborated with peers.

It seems past students had either very different views from present students or did not anticipate how students would actually behave under the Free Collaboration policy. I'll keep that in mind if I query present students about how to further evolve course policies.

General Comments

  • The course is making me a good programmer, but I feel like it could also make me a good systems thinker.

    Be patient as these are not separate skills. Once you've seen how enough modest-sized programs get put together, you'll be able to see how larger programs can be assembled and how groups of programs get assembled. That's a long of training: musicians practice scales, and etudes, then work on concertos; painters practice sketches and brushwork then move on to portraits and murals; athletes drill footwork and coordination, then go on to match strategy. It's all important but you'll have a greater appreciation for the birds-eye view later if you get acquainted with the details now.

More importantly, the only way to learn how to code larger things is to practice coding larger things. For veteran, the projects in this class are single-sitting endeavors, small programs, of little consequence. If you continue building your skill and endurance, you'll see how small they are within a few years.

  • …the exams are stressful. Being weighted at 20% each and only 20% of this class being given away for free, that's a lot of pressure being handed out every single exam.

  • If the goal is punishing students who don't learn the content and only use AI to fully offset knowledge, a lesser portion of our grades going to our exams can still achieve this goal. [Gives example weighting of projects worth 5% instead of 2% as evidence].

    Prior to Spring 2026, out-of class work was worth more in my courses. Some students cheated and some of them were caught and disciplined. With the advent of AI, it is now intractable to determine when a student does out-of-class work by their own effort or outsources it to an LLM. This leads to students who are working hard getting lower scores on out-class-assignments than those who simply ask the LLM to do it for them. That's the "given away for free" aspect the first commenter mentioned. This is a deeply unfair scheme: folks who play by the rules and can't complete an assignment get lower scores than those that feed it to Chat-gippity or friends and submit it as their own work. The best remedy I know for this is to discount work we can't verify is legit and measure the understanding students accrete through their effort in a monitored exam setting. It's not a perfect solution as many students have commented: students experience various levels of exam stress. But my direct experience is that it actually doesn't make much difference in overall grades:

    • Fall 2025 forbade use of AI on assignments and used an older grading scheme which assigned more weight to out-of-class work; the Overall Median scores was 87.75%
    • Spring 2026 used the scheme we have now with exams up-weighted; the Overall Median scores 86.53%
    • Both semesters had a standard deviation of about 11%.

    There was no statistical difference in the overall grade distribution based on the two different grading schemes. The biggest difference is this:

    • Fall 2025 had 39 Academic Integrity Violations, about 10% of students, requiring dozens of hours of staff time to process and a huge amount of work for the Office of Student Conduct.
    • Spring 2026 had 0 Academic Integrity Violations

    The tradeoffs are crystal clear to me with the current policies: the best measure of student understanding I have (exams scores) are not affected by putting more emphasis on exams, student grades are not impacted overall, students can determine what level of AI use they benefit from outside of exams, and staff are saved a tremendous amount time policing students. I'm happy to talk about this but I've thought about this a LOT and it's going to take more than a "this is stressful" or "I think the weights should be different" to convince me to change these policies.

  • I… have a hard time learning in lectures and discussion. We go a little fast for my pace and I would likely have an easier time just going through lecture slides on my own time.

    This is normal. It is a misconception that sitting in lecture is enough to absorb and master course content. When you think you've got a handle on a lecture topic, explain it to your favorite stuffed animal. If you find that your stuffed animal is skeptical of your explanation, then likely you aren't as solid on it as possible but the act of saying things out loud magically gives insight into what you might look at next to get more grounded. Repeat until you can teach someone else, stuffed or not, what you learned as that's the surest sign of mastery.
